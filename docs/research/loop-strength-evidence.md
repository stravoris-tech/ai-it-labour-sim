# Outside evidence sets four loop strengths

Outside evidence supports one loop that is material, one that needs restructuring more than a number, and two that are close to zero. **R7 (GCC captive scale) is the strongest.** A base kR7 of about 0.4 (range 0.15-0.8, hard ceiling about 1.0) amplifies any exogenous push toward GCCs by roughly 1.5x, and the high case reaches 3x. **R6 (vendor capacity exit and pricing rules) is real, but its spec shape is wrong.** Cuts start on relative margin shortfall (about 0.8 of normal margin), not on absolute distress. AI investment rises under margin pressure rather than falling, and the main way failing viability hurts a vendor is client flight, not excess layoffs. **R10 (talent poaching penalty on vendor choice) is near zero** (base 0.10, low 0, high 0.30). The 2022 attrition peak, at 1.6-2.3x the 2024 level, coincided with the best growth year in a decade, and its documented effects were costs and capacity limits, not client switching. **R4 (skill erosion speeding exit from IT) is also weak** (base 0.10, low 0, high 0.25-0.30). The monthly US hazard of leaving the labour force barely moves with spell length (elasticity about 0.03-0.04), and it falls in cyclical slumps. None of these ranges comes from an econometric estimate of the exact parameter. Each one translates case data, flow statistics and industry series into logit or elasticity units, and the translation steps are spelled out below so the model team can check or override them. The practical upshot is that sensitivity analysis matters most for R7 and the R6 pricing bounds. R10 and R4 can probably sit at their base values without changing 2040 conclusions, though that should be confirmed with a 0/base/high sweep.

## R7: captive scale earns a 0.4 logit gain per doubling, capped well below tipping

### GCC growth has run ahead of vendors for fifteen years, mostly inside existing centres

India's captive base grew from more than **500 captives in 2008** ([CIO.com](https://www.cio.com/article/282074/outsourcing-the-captive-model-for-offshoring-is-thriving-says-research-firm.html)) to **2,117 GCCs, 3,728 units and about 2.36M employees in FY26** ([CXOToday](https://cxotoday.com/others/from-delivery-engines-to-ai-nerve-centers-indias-gccs-take-the-global-lead/); [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/news/story/indias-gccs-move-from-cost-centres-to-global-innovation-hubs-zinnov-550459-2026-08-20)). The key differential for calibration is **GCC headcount growth of 12% a year over 2010-2023 against 8% for the top five Indian vendors, and revenue growth of about 11% against 8% over FY15-FY23** ([Motilal Oswal](https://motilaloswal.com/site/rreports/HTML/638581016141322416/index.htm)). That gap widened sharply after 2023. GCCs took **over 100,000 of about 126,000 net new tech jobs in FY25** ([India Briefing](https://www.india-briefing.com/news/indias-workforce-trends-shift-toward-gccs-over-it-firms-36459.html/)). In the same year, software exports grew 12.7% while the forex revenue of 64 listed IT services groups grew only 3.8% ([The Ken](https://the-ken.com/the-collection/indias-it-services-firms-are-being-disrupted-by-their-own-playbook/)).

The evidence that matters for a scale loop is where the growth happened. Count growth ran at about 5-7% a year, headcount growth at 11-12%. Over FY19-FY24 the number of GCCs rose 32%, units about 60%, and **average centre size 24%** ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/economy/indias-position-as-gcc-capital-of-the-world-nasscom-zinnov-study/2268673/)). About 1,125 units were added against roughly 415 net new parent GCCs, so at least 60% of new units were second or later sites of existing parents (derived from the same series). Expansions made up 36% of setup and expansion events in CY22 and 44% in CY23 ([Motilal Oswal](https://motilaloswal.com/site/rreports/HTML/638581016141322416/index.htm)), and 57% in 2010 ([CIO.com](https://www.cio.com/article/282074/outsourcing-the-captive-model-for-offshoring-is-thriving-says-research-firm.html)). Expansions are larger than first setups. Simple decomposition arithmetic therefore implies that existing centres supplied roughly 65-85% of headcount growth. No source publishes that split directly.

Two further signals point to learning rather than just accumulation. At firm level, **Barclays' India tech workforce moved from about one-third to about two-thirds in-house over a decade, and RBL Bank's in-house to outsourced split moved from 35:65 to 60:40** ([The Ken](https://the-ken.com/the-collection/indias-it-services-firms-are-being-disrupted-by-their-own-playbook/)). At ecosystem level, **centres set up between 2002 and 2015 took 5-10 years to mature, while those set up since FY21 take under 5 years** ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/news/story/indias-gccs-move-from-cost-centres-to-global-innovation-hubs-zinnov-550459-2026-08-20)). That halving coincided with the base roughly doubling. It is the cleanest evidence of the sector-level effect a kR7 term represents, which runs through deeper talent pools, setup playbooks and setup-as-a-service providers. Academic work supports path dependence at firm level. Albertoni, Elia and Piscitello studied 410 firms over 2006-2011 and found that firms repeat earlier entry modes, with stronger learning when the repeated mode is captive. They publish no effect size ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v103y2019icp530-546.html)). Saturation is far off: only about **25% of the Forbes Global 2000 have India operations** ([CXOToday](https://cxotoday.com/others/from-delivery-engines-to-ai-nerve-centers-indias-gccs-take-the-global-lead/)).

### Translating share drift into kR7 depends on how much of the drift is credited to the loop

In a logit, adding kR7 x (GCCW/GCCW_2024 - 1) to V_GCC means a doubling of GCC work share multiplies GCC odds by e^kR7. Linearised around the 2024 share s0, the loop gain is G = kR7 x (1 - s0), and an exogenous push to GCC log-odds is amplified by 1/(1 - G). Stability requires G < 1. With s0 between 0.10 and 0.30, that means **kR7 below about 1.1-1.4**, a hard ceiling that holds whatever the data say.

The macro back-out uses the revenue differential. GCC revenue growing at 11.4% against 8% for vendors over eight years raises the GCC-to-vendor ratio by about 1.28x, a log-odds change of 0.25. Over the same period GCC share of the pool rose from about 18% to 22%, a relative rise of about 0.22. If a fraction f of the log-odds drift is caused by the scale loop, kR7 is about 1.14 x f. The share of growth coming from existing centres (65-85%) is a loose upper bound on f. Much of that within-firm growth is exogenous demand (cost arbitrage, AI and digital mandates, US visa policy) landing at sites that already exist. A defensible f is 0.2-0.5, which gives kR7 of about 0.25-0.55. The micro check gives an upper bound. If Barclays' and RBL's shifts were entirely caused by the loop, the implied kR7 would be about 1.39 and 1.45. But these are hand-picked firms, include deliberate strategy changes, and sit at firm level, while the model term is sector-level and diluted by the many firms that have no GCC. Taking 30-50% of those values gives 0.4-0.7, consistent with the macro method.

| kR7 | Attribution assumption | Odds multiplier at doubling | Loop gain at s0 = 0.15 | Long-run amplification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low 0.15 | f about 0.15. Most drift is exogenous. Weights the 2000s divestiture record. | 1.16 | 0.13 | about 1.15x |
| Base 0.40 | f about 0.35. Consistent with the macro back-out, firm cases discounted about 70%, and the maturity-speed evidence. | 1.49 | 0.34 | about 1.5x |
| High 0.80 | f about 0.7. Post-2020 acceleration treated as mostly self-reinforcing. | 2.23 | 0.68 | about 3x |

**Confidence: medium-low.** That the loop is positive is well supported. Its size rests almost entirely on the judgement-based attribution fraction f, and no panel regression of sector captive share on lagged captive share exists. Treat the high case as a stress test near the tipping region, and reject values above about 1.0.

### Divestitures and parent stress argue for a capped, reversible form

The loop has run backwards before. GE, British Airways and Dun & Bradstreet all converted their 1990s captives to third-party providers, and **about 20% of captives had downsized by 2010** ([CIO.com](https://www.cio.com/article/282074/outsourcing-the-captive-model-for-offshoring-is-thriving-says-research-firm.html)). In 2008-2009 Citi sold its BPO captive to TCS and its IT unit to Wipro, each paired with a buy-back contract, and Aviva, AXA and Fidelity sold or shopped captives as well ([CIO.com 2009](https://www.cio.com/article/278296/outsourcing-outsourcing-the-demise-of-the-offshore-captive-center.html); [Skadden](https://skadden.com/about/news-and-rankings/news/2008/12/citigroup-sells-subsidiary-to-wipro-signs-outsourc)). Divestitures cluster among **mid-sized captives of 500-1,000 FTE**, and more than 55% of captive acquisitions are made by vendors already serving the parent ([SSON / Everest](https://www.ssonetwork.com/service-delivery-transformation-bpo/articles/captive-acquisitions-by-service-providers-who-bene)). The pattern points to a threshold: sub-scale captives get sold under parent cash stress, while large ones keep compounding.

Five form changes follow. First, make the term concave: kR7 x ln(GCCW/GCCW_2024) behaves like the linear term near the 2024 level, gives 0.69 x kR7 at a doubling, and does not grow without bound. Second, add a **stability cap**. Cap the term at a fixed number of logit units to reflect talent-pool and wage-inflation limits, and enforce kR7 x (1 - s0) below about 0.7 so that no parameter sweep lands in runaway territory. The cap level is a design judgement, not a sourced number. Third, allow the loop to reverse under parent financial stress, consistent with 2008-2009. Fourth, consider sector-specific kR7 that is higher for BFSI and software, where GCC owners dominate spend, and lower for manufacturing and energy. Fifth, and most important for calibration, refit the exogenous GCC trend jointly with the loop so that the trend absorbs (1 - f) of historical drift. Otherwise adding kR7 on top of a trend already fitted to history will overshoot.

## R10: the 2022 attrition spike left costs and capacity limits but no client defections

### Attrition nearly doubled, and growth was the best in a decade

Attrition across ICRA's sample of Indian IT services firms rose from **about 18% before the pandemic to about 23% at the Q4 FY22-Q1 FY23 peak, then fell to about 13% from Q3 FY24** ([ICRA](https://www.icra.in/Rating/DownloadResearchSpecialCommentReport?id=6040)). At individual firms the peak reached **Infosys 28.4%, HCLTech 23.8%, Wipro 23.3% and TCS 21.5%** ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/it-firms-headed-for-lower-attrition-on-fears-of-global-recession-say-analysts-news-230336)). Cognizant hit 33% annualised and 24% LTM in Q3 2021 ([MarketBeat transcript](https://www.marketbeat.com/earnings/reports/2021-10-26-cognizant-technology-solutions-co-stock)), and Accenture reached 19% in fiscal 2022 ([Accenture 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1467373/000146737322000295/acn-20220831.htm)). FY24 values were 12.5-14.2% ([Angel One](https://www.angelone.in/news/share-market/attrition-rates-of-top-3-large-cap-it-companies-in-india)). Against the model's 2024 normalisation, **the 2022 peak is a ratio r of about 1.6-2.3, with the industry at about 1.8**. The spike was synchronised across firms: vendor-specific deviations were about +/-5 points.

The cost channel is well documented. ICRA's sample operating margin fell from 26.4% in FY21 to 22.9% in FY23, and employee cost rose from 52.3% of revenue in FY19 to 58.0% in FY24 ([ICRA](https://www.icra.in/Rating/DownloadResearchSpecialCommentReport?id=6040)). Infosys's Q4 FY22 bridge attributes **-160 bps to compensation and subcontractors and -60 bps to lower utilisation** ([ICICI Direct](https://www.icicidirect.com/research/equity/blog/infosys-q4fy22-q4-numbers-weak-on-margins-fy23-guidance-reflects-cost-pressures)). The supply-side hit was roughly 100-200 bps of margin per firm, and it was partly passed through, since **clients accepted 4-7% rate rises for in-demand skills** ([ISG](https://isg-one.com/articles/index-insider-top-5-ways-enterprises-are-addressing-attrition)). The margin trough lagged the attrition peak by 2-4 quarters, and employee cost share kept rising after attrition fell. That points to sticky wage resets rather than attrition itself.

The client-preference channel, which is what kR10 measures, is weak. Everest's survey of 496 executives found talent management was the top pain point, yet **overall satisfaction with providers rose 5% in 2021** ([Outsource Accelerator on Everest](https://news.outsourceaccelerator.com/attrition-in-it-providers-is-the-biggest-pain-point-for-clients-everest-survey/)). ISG found that clients responded with attrition SLAs, project delays, price acceptance, more subcontracting, and in 25% of cases faster automation. ISG called switching providers "likely problematic" because attrition was industry-wide ([ISG](https://isg-one.com/articles/index-insider-top-5-ways-enterprises-are-addressing-attrition)). The firm with the highest attrition grew fastest: **Infosys grew 19.7% in constant currency in FY22 at 27% attrition** ([ICICI Direct](https://www.icicidirect.com/research/equity/blog/infosys-q4fy22-q4-numbers-weak-on-margins-fy23-guidance-reflects-cost-pressures)), and then an industry-leading 15.4% in FY23 ([Hubbis](https://hubbis.com/news/infosys-industry-leading-fy23-revenue-growth-of-15-4-with-healthy-21-0-operating-margins)). ICRA sample growth peaked near 20% in Q1 FY22 and fell to about zero in Q3 FY24, after attrition had already normalised ([ICRA](https://www.icra.in/Rating/DownloadResearchSpecialCommentReport?id=6040)). That is the opposite sign to a strong poaching-to-lost-business loop, and reverse causality explains it: demand booms cause poaching. The one real commercial effect was supply rationing. Cognizant's CEO said the firm lacked "enough headcount to fulfill our potential" and had to choose "which clients to serve, which deals to chase" ([MarketBeat](https://www.marketbeat.com/earnings/reports/2021-10-26-cognizant-technology-solutions-co-stock)). That effect belongs in a capacity constraint, not in the logit.

GCC poaching is structural rather than spike-driven. GCCs pay **up to 20% more** than IT services ([BusinessWorld](https://businessworld.in/article/gccs-offer-20-higher-salaries-than-it-services-report-530907)), or up to 14% more for some executives ([Nearshore Americas](https://nearshoreamericas.com/multinationals-wage-talent-war-for-their-captive-centers-in-india/)). The 20-40% premium sometimes quoted could not be confirmed. No source gives the share of GCC hires that come from vendors.

### A doubling of attrition should cut vendor odds by about 10%, not more

Lowering V_v by kR10 x (r - 1) multiplies vendor v's odds by e^(-kR10 x (r - 1)). At a doubling, kR10 values of 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30 and 0.50 cut odds by 5%, 10%, 18%, 26% and 39%.

The low case (**kR10 = 0, plausible 0-0.03**) holds that clients do not penalise attrition as long as delivery holds. It fits the satisfaction rise and the growth record, and it is the right choice if the model already has separate capacity and cost-to-price channels.

The base case (**0.10**) allows a small real penalty consistent with SLAs, evaluations of alternatives at the margin, and faster insourcing. At the 2022 industry ratio of 1.8 it implies about an 8% odds cut, which would be invisible against 15-20% demand-driven growth and so matches the record. In value terms this is comparable to a few percent of price, about the size of the 4-7% rate rises clients accepted. That equivalence depends on the model's price coefficient, which should be checked.

The high case (**0.30**) is only defensible if margin, quality and capacity effects are all folded into the logit with no separate channel, or for a vendor-specific attrition shock where switching to rivals actually becomes possible. Values above about 0.5 contradict the FY22 record.

**Confidence: medium that the loop is small, low on any precise value.** No study identifies the causal effect of vendor attrition on client choice.

### Smooth the signal, give no reward below baseline, and re-check the 2024 anchor

Four design changes follow from the evidence. First, **smooth Attr_v** with a 2-4 quarter first-order delay, matching the 2-4 quarter lag between the attrition peak and the margin trough. Second, **floor the term at zero for r < 1**. Nothing in the record shows below-baseline attrition winning share, and the 2024 trough would otherwise reward vendors simply for a soft hiring market. Third, recognise that the 2024 anchor is a post-spike low. A return to the roughly 18% pre-pandemic rate registers as r of about 1.3-1.4 and would impose a penalty that was never observed in 2019. Either normalise on a multi-year average or set a dead band up to r of about 1.4. Fourth, route the documented effects through their own channels: a 100-200 bps margin cost per doubling, and a fulfilment cap. When all vendors spike together, a logit penalty mostly shifts share from vendors as a class to in-house and GCC options. That double-counts the structural GCC shift already in R7 and the exogenous trend.

## R6: margin stress triggers early, modest cuts, more AI spend and client flight

### Cuts start on relative shortfall at healthy margins and run 1-4% of headcount

Across 25 years, large vendors cut headcount when margins slipped 1-2 points below target and growth stalled. Their absolute margins were still far above any distress level. **Accenture cut 19,000 people (about 2.5%) in March 2023**, alongside an operating margin guidance cut to 14.1-14.7% from 15.3-15.5% that included the charges, executing over 18 months ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2023/03/24/accenture_19000_layoffs/); [CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/Accenture-AI-investment-double-workforce-layoffs/652867/)). EDS announced cuts of 3-4% within one to two months of lowering targets in 2002 ([CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2002/10/30/technology/eds/index.htm)). Cognizant set a 22% margin target in February 2017, about one quarter after Elliott's entry, with reported cuts of up to about 4% ([FinTech Futures](https://www.fintechfutures.com/venture-capital-funding/cognizant-plans-layoffs-automation-investment-in-digital-and-m-a)). It cut about 1% under NextGen in 2023 ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/cognizant-warns-of-revenue-decline-in-current-fiscal-plans-to-lay-off-3-500-employees-news-283514)). **TCS cut about 2% (12,200) in July 2025** after a 3.1% constant-currency revenue fall ([Gulf News / Reuters](https://gulfnews.com/business/markets/ai-overhaul-indias-tcs-to-cut-12000-jobs-this-year-after-growth-slows-in-q1-1.500212931)). As of August 2026 it remains the only Indian vendor to announce mass AI-era layoffs ([Deccan Chronicle / Reuters](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/technology/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-1981013)). Capgemini France cut up to 7% with its margin below 10% against an 11-12% target ([HRKatha](https://www.hrkatha.com/news/capgemini-to-cut-up-to-2400-roles-in-france-as-ai-shift-reshapes-operations/)). Indian majors mostly adjust through intake rather than layoffs. **The top five lost a net 69,167 staff in FY24** (Infosys -25,994, Wipro -24,516) on flat revenue ([Business Today](https://businesstoday.in/technology/news/story/top-5-indian-it-companies-lost-69167-employees-in-fy24-report-427193-2024-04-27)), repairing utilisation toward the 76-80% band Infosys called comfortable after a trough near 73% ([Infosys Q3 FY10 transcript](https://www.infosys.com/investors/reports-filings/quarterly-results/2009-2010/Q3/Documents/transcripts/press-conference.pdf)).

When viability genuinely fails, capacity falls fast, but mostly in line with demand. **Satyam fell from about 50,000 to about 24,000 staff in two years**, while customers fell from about 650 to about 350, and more than 80% of departing clients took their project teams with them ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/article/beyond-business/back-from-the-brink-111011500025_1.html)). Atos cut 19% to 63,193 as core organic revenue fell 16.2% ([MarketScreener / Reuters](https://au.marketscreener.com/news/atos-hits-yearly-revenue-target-after-restructuring-slashed-19-of-workforce-ce7e5fd8d989f027)). That is only about 3 points of excess, before perimeter effects. DXC's headcount fell more slowly than its revenue, according to a secondary source ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DXC_Technology)).

The dominant distress channel is demand. **Atos book-to-bill fell to 73% from 93% because clients waited for its refinancing, while its renewal rate held at 88%** ([Atos H1 2024](https://www.atosgroup.com/en/press/atos-reports-first-half-2024-results)). Distress hits new bookings first and renewals later. Natural attrition keeps flowing throughout (14-15% at Atos), so a large share of capacity exit takes care of itself.

### AI investment rises under margin pressure, the opposite of the spec

The spec assumes AI investment falls as viability falls. The record shows the reverse across the margin-defence range. Within three months of its 19,000 cuts, **Accenture committed $3B over three years to data and AI** and planned to double AI-skilled staff to 80,000 ([CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/Accenture-AI-investment-double-workforce-layoffs/652867/)). In 2025 it ran an approximately $865M restructuring while AI and data specialists nearly doubled to 77,000, and its CEO called AI "expansionary" ([Yahoo Finance / Reuters](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/accenture-cutting-staff-cant-retrain-063900475.html)). Cognizant 2017, TCS 2025 and Capgemini 2026 all paired cuts with automation or AI redeployment. They cut non-billable staff, management layers and real estate instead. Even distressed Atos raised capex by EUR 168M year on year while free cash flow ran at -EUR 1.9B ([Atos H1 2024](https://atosgroup.com/en/press/atos-reports-first-half-2024-results)). The evidence of explicit AI budget cuts under cash constraint is absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. A fall should therefore be confined to a deep-distress tail.

### Price floors bend toward expected cost, and AI discount asks cluster at 20-30%

Below-cost bidding happens deal by deal but not across a whole portfolio. **HCLTech's E.ON deal charges nothing in year one**. Rivals price on assumed 70-80% productivity gains over five to seven years, which TechM's CEO called "irrational", while Infosys walks away from uneconomic contracts ([Deccan Chronicle / Reuters](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/technology/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-1981013)). Infosys lost parts of contracts in 2026 to bids that built expected AI gains into price ([CXO Digitalpulse](https://www.cxodigitalpulse.com/infosys-reportedly-loses-parts-of-three-major-contracts-amid-it-services-price-competition/)). In 2015 some bids came in **as much as 40% below competitors** ([CIO](https://www.cio.com/article/246641/the-it-outsourcing-price-wars-are-on.html)). Even so, portfolio margins stayed positive at Atos (2.3%) and the distressed Satyam ran at 5.7-8.3% EBITDA. So the floor binds near delivery cost over a contract's life, measured against expected rather than current cost.

On the ceiling, client asks run **20-30% on new contracts** ([Gulf News / Reuters](https://gulfnews.com/business/markets/ai-overhaul-indias-tcs-to-cut-12000-jobs-this-year-after-growth-slows-in-q1-1.500212931)), and Persistent's CEO cites 25-30% ([Deccan Chronicle / Reuters](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/technology/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-1981013)). Past episodes were 5-20% in 2009 ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/outsourcing-prices-falling-by-up-to-20-in-downturn-say-analysts)) and up to 40% in 2015. Realised market deflation is far smaller. **Kotak estimates 3-3.5% a year of AI-led revenue deflation for Indian IT through FY28** ([ANI](https://www.aninews.in/news/business/ai-could-drive-3-35-annual-revenue-deflation-in-indian-it-services-through-fy28-kotak20260825140021/)). The gap is explained by partial repricing each year and partial AI exposure, even though HFS reports renegotiation now happens within 24 months of signing ([HFS](https://www.hfsresearch.com/news/ai-drives-it-contract-renegotiations-within-24-months-as-pricing-models-shift/)).

Margin should be smoothed over **0.5-1.0 years**, to match the one-to-two-quarter lag between a guidance cut and a layoff announcement, with a further 12-18 month execution delay. **Confidence: medium** on the relative threshold, the timing and the discount ceiling, which have consistent multi-episode support. **Low** on the layoff slope and the price floor, which are synthesised from a handful of cases with no econometric estimate.

### Four structural changes matter more than the parameter values

First, reverse the AI-investment response: hold it flat or **raise it by up to about 20% as viability falls to about 0.5**, then cut it steeply below about 0.3, where cash becomes the constraint. Second, add a **viability-to-client channel**: low viability lowers the new-bid win rate first (the Atos book-to-bill drop) and renewal or retention later (the Satyam client exodus with teams). This channel moves far more capacity than excess layoffs do. Third, implement extra exit mostly as reduced hiring and replacement, not layoffs alone, because Indian vendors shrink through intake. Fourth, make the price floor depend on viability, for example floor = 1.0 - 0.15 x (1 - viability) below threshold, and define it against expected delivery cost. The AI discount ceiling should apply per repricing event on AI-exposed work, with realised deflation of about 3-3.5% a year used as a calibration check rather than a reason to raise the ceiling.

## R4: exit hazards barely move with spell length, so skill erosion acts through hiring

### The monthly hazard of dropping out is nearly flat and falls in recessions

BLS flows by duration for 2014 show the monthly probability that an unemployed person leaves the labour force at **23% under 5 weeks, 22% at 5-26 weeks, and 25-26% at 27+ weeks**. Over the same range, job finding falls from 35% to 11% ([BLS Spotlight](https://www.bls.gov/spotlight/2015/long-term-unemployment/)). A log-log fit gives an elasticity of the exit hazard with respect to elapsed duration of **about 0.03-0.04**, with a modest step after six months. Cumulatively, the long-term unemployed are more often out of the labour force 15 months later (**33.7% vs 27.2%**) ([Krueger, Cramer and Cho](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2014a_Krueger.pdf)). That is an implied cumulative elasticity of about 0.13. A stock-flow model with a constant hazard already generates most of it mechanically, because longer spells mean longer exposure. The cyclical sign is negative. Aggregate monthly flows from unemployment to non-participation fell from about 20% in 2008 to 14% in 2009 ([Kroft, Lange, Notowidigdo and Katz](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2014/IPR-WP-14-25.pdf)), and the long-term unemployed's exit rate fell with the unemployment rate (coefficient -0.073) ([Krueger et al.](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2014a_Krueger.pdf)). An exit multiplier driven by aggregate Dur therefore fights the cyclical evidence.

Occupation-switching data tell the same story. In US SIPP data, **44.4% of unemployed workers change major occupation at re-employment, rising to 53.7% for spells of 9+ months**, about 1.7 points per extra month ([Carrillo-Tudela and Visschers](https://docs.iza.org/dp13307.pdf)). The per-spell elasticity is about 0.14. Once falling job finding is netted out, however, the per-month hazard of leaving the occupation is flat to slightly declining (derived). Tech-specific outcomes show stress in pay rather than in exit. About 45% of re-employed 2022 big-tech layoffs left the tech industry, but many took tech roles in other industries ([Revelio Labs via Stacker](https://stacker.com/business-economy/where-laid-tech-workers-are-going)). The January 2026 Displaced Worker Survey shows **information-industry displaced workers 76.8% employed and 15.1% out of the labour force**, but only 49% of re-employed workers matching their old pay, against 62% two years earlier ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/disp_08272026.htm)).

Skill erosion is real but shows up in hiring and wages. **Callbacks fall about 45% over the first eight months of unemployment and then flatten** ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/dependence-duration-and-labor-market-conditions-evidence-united-states)). Each extra month of non-employment cuts re-employment wages by **about 0.8%** ([Schmieder, von Wachter and Bender](https://docs.iza.org/dp8700.pdf)). IT skills turn over fast: **47% of computer and math vacancies needed at least one new skill in 2019 versus 2007**, and the share of CS and engineering graduates working in related jobs falls from 58% at age 26 to 41% at age 50 ([Microeconomic Insights on Deming and Noray](https://microeconomicinsights.org/?p=2955)). The popular "2.5-year half-life of tech skills" has no traceable primary source. India's evidence is a queue rather than a spell. At least **10,000 freshers waited up to two years for onboarding**, and Infosys graduate intake fell 76% to 11,900 ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/it-giants-infosys-wipro-tcs-postpone-onboarding-of-over-10000-graduates)). Offer-holders tend to wait rather than leave, so there is no basis for an India value above the onshore one in the first year.

### kR4 belongs near 0.1, ideally inside a threshold-saturation form

**Low kR4 = 0** fits the flat monthly hazard, the declining occupation-exit hazard and the negative cyclical sign. The stock-flow structure still raises cumulative exit as spells lengthen. **Base kR4 = 0.10** sits above the measured monthly elasticity of 0.03-0.04, allowing for structural decline in an AI-hit field, where net mobility out of the occupation rises and obsolescence runs faster than in 2007-2019. It stays below the cumulative elasticity of about 0.13 to avoid double-counting exposure time. As a power law, 0.10 gives about +7% exit at 2x Dur and +15% at 4x. **High kR4 = 0.25-0.30** applies only if the base exit rate is calibrated on short-spell seekers and Dur is meant to stand in for discouragement in a structurally shrinking field. No source supports values near 1. **Confidence: medium-high** that the loop is small. The main uncertainty is whether AI-era obsolescence will break the 2007-2019 pattern, and no post-2023 measurement exists yet.

The evidence favours a **threshold-plus-saturation form** over a power law. The data show no change below about six months, a step of +10-25% between 6 and 12 months, and flattening beyond 12-15 months: callbacks are flat after eight months ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/dependence-duration-and-labor-market-conditions-evidence-united-states)) and job finding is "largely flat" beyond 12-15 months ([FRBSF](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/long-term-unemployment)). A candidate is multiplier = 1 + m_max x max(0, Dur - Dur_th)/(Dur - Dur_th + Dur_half), with Dur_th about 6 months and Dur_half about 6 months in absolute terms. Setting m_max to 0 (low), 0.2 (base) or 0.5 (high) gives +0%, +10% or +25% at 12 months and +0%, +15% or +37.5% at 24 months, which roughly matches the power-law range above. If the model already has duration-dependent job finding, keep kR4 at the low end, because that is where skill erosion actually operates. If AI-driven exit from IT is the goal, a separate demand-gap or "field outlook" driver is better supported than a Dur elasticity.

The design changes to the prototype forms are as follows. For **R7**, switch to a log (concave) term with a stability cap, keep kR7 x (1 - s0) below about 0.7, allow reversal under parent stress, consider sector-specific values, and refit the exogenous GCC trend jointly. For **R10**, smooth Attr_v over 2-4 quarters, floor the term at zero below baseline (no reward), add a dead band or multi-year anchor because 2024 is a trough, and move margin and capacity effects to separate channels. For **R6**, define viability relative to each vendor's normal margin, reverse the AI-investment sign above deep distress, add a viability-to-win-rate and retention channel, implement exit mainly as reduced intake, and make the price floor viability-dependent and tied to expected cost. For **R4**, replace the power law with a threshold-saturation multiplier in absolute months, keep the India value at or below onshore, and prefer a structural field-outlook driver for AI-driven exit.

## Conclusion

The four loops are not alike, and the evidence does more than set numbers. It changes which loops deserve modelling attention. R7 is the only one where outside data imply a gain large enough to reshape 2040 outcomes, and its uncertainty is concentrated in one judgement: how much of fifteen years of GCC share drift to credit to self-reinforcement rather than exogenous cost and mandate pressure. That makes the joint refit of trend and loop, not the choice of kR7, the decision with most leverage. R6's biggest finding is a sign error. A vendor under margin pressure in the AI era does not starve its automation budget. It funds automation by cutting people and real estate, which feeds the productivity loop rather than damping it. Meanwhile the real viability spiral runs through clients deferring awards, not through layoffs.

R10 and R4 share a lesson. Both describe effects that are visible in the data (margin squeeze from attrition, worse re-employment after long spells), but those effects sit in channels other than the ones the prototype equations use: costs and capacity for attrition, job finding and wages for skill erosion. Wiring them into choice values or exit multipliers mostly double-counts. The efficient test is a 0/base/high sweep on both. If 2035-2040 conclusions do not move, the identification gap does not matter, and modelling effort is better spent on R7's cap and R6's pricing bounds, which do bind.
