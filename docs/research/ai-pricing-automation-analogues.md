# AI deflation shrinks billable units before rate cards

Generative AI is cutting IT services prices mainly through fewer billable units, renewal step-downs and productivity commitments written into annuity deals. It is not, so far, cutting hourly or per-FTE rates. The large Indian vendors now openly accept AI deflation of about **2-3% a year on their own books and 3-5% a year on the AI-exposed part of the industry**. Renewals are stepping down about 20%, against client asks of 25-30% less for the same work. Hard data on T&M and staff-augmentation rate cards is essentially missing, and the one vendor that comments on it, Infosys, says pricing has stayed stable. Past automation cycles show the same pattern. Hours fall long before rates do. The billable hour and per-unit pricing survive for a decade or more by absorbing discounts. Labour adjusts first through junior intake rather than layoffs. And the largest long-run threat is the client doing the work itself, not a vendor price cut. The IT offshoring wave, the legal billable hour and translation under machine translation are the closest analogues. Switchboards, ATMs, typing pools, bookkeeping and CAD mostly set outer bounds on lags and show how role mixes shift. For the model, the evidence supports keeping T&M and staff-augmentation **rates** free of forward price pressure, but it argues against a blanket exemption. Large effort-priced annuity contracts are exactly where clients are reopening deals and writing in forward productivity commitments, and in every past episode buyers found ways to price expected productivity into effort contracts.

## Vendors now concede 2-5% a year of AI deflation, mostly at renewal

The clearest evidence comes from the April 2026 (Q4 FY26) earnings calls of the Indian majors. HCLTech is the only firm that has put a number on it. Its CEO said **"40% of the industry runs the risk of being disrupted by AI and can shrink 3% to 5% CAGR for a few years"**, which "would translate to 2% to 3% for our portfolio" ([Zerodha Daily Brief](https://thedailybrief.zerodha.com/p/has-ai-broken-the-indian-it-model)). Kotak Institutional Equities called HCLTech the first company to quantify industry AI deflation and uses **3.5% pricing erosion** as its base case ([Tribune/ANI](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/business/it-services-growth-to-stay-range-bound-at-3-per-cent-as-ai-deflation-pressures-margins-mid-tiers-better-placed-says-kotak/)). One press report described HCLTech's guidance as its own revenue falling 3-5%. The transcript-based compilation shows that figure belongs to the AI-exposed industry segment, so 2-3% is the right number for the vendor's own book ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/04/28/tcs_infosys_wipro_hcl_fy26/)).

The deal-level evidence is consistent with those annual rates. HCLTech said a deal that would have been $100M now closes at about **$80M**, and that holding total contract value (TCV) flat "does require at least 25%, 30% more effort to convert". It also said it walked away from more than $1B of deals that "will not make sense" ([Zerodha Daily Brief](https://thedailybrief.zerodha.com/p/has-ai-broken-the-indian-it-model)). Persistent Systems says clients want **the same work for 25-30% less**. Reuters also reports that some rivals assume **70-80% productivity gains over 5-7 years** when they bid ([Reuters via Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/business/indian-sub-continent/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-462989)). Set the 20% realised step-down against the 25-30% ask, and vendors appear to be conceding roughly 70-80% of what clients demand. A 20% step-down spread over a five- to seven-year contract is about 3-4% a year, which matches the HCLTech and Kotak annual figures.

Infosys supplies the most important qualitative statement. Its CFO said **"the productivity will get passed back to the client largely"**, and that "most of our growth this year has been pricing-led because the volumes have been softer" ([Zerodha Daily Brief](https://thedailybrief.zerodha.com/p/has-ai-broken-the-indian-it-model)). Its FY2026 Form 20-F adds that "clients have started demanding higher commitments around productivity improvements" and that Infosys is "committing to higher productivity improvements in our contracts". It also lists "AI led compression" and client demand for outcome-based pricing as risks ([Infosys Form 20-F FY2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001067491/000119312526270520/infy-20260331.htm)). TCS's CEO called deflation "degrowth" after FY26 revenue slipped about 0.5% ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/04/28/tcs_infosys_wipro_hcl_fy26/)). Over April to June 2026, TCS, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech grew 1-3% while Persistent grew 16% and Coforge about 33%. That points to share shift toward mid-tier firms as well as overall market deflation ([Reuters via Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/business/indian-sub-continent/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-462989)).

The timing of the pressure matters for a stock-and-flow model. HFS Research reports that IT services contracts are being **reopened within 24 months of signing** rather than at renewal, with deals above $50M ACV under the most pressure. Its reasoning is that most large outsourcing contracts were signed in 2024 and early 2025, before agentic AI changed delivery economics ([HFS Research](https://www.hfsresearch.com/news/ai-drives-it-contract-renegotiations-within-24-months-as-pricing-models-shift/)). In HFS's 2026 survey of 605 G2000 executives, **62% said they renegotiated with IT service providers**, against 75% with BPO providers and 72% with software vendors ([HFS webinar](https://www.hfsresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf/HFS-Webinar-Buying-SaS_21-07-2026.pdf)). ISG describes "financial engineering happening on deals to bring these savings forward faster", meaning price cuts arrive before the productivity that is supposed to fund them ([ISG Index Insider 1H26](https://isg-one.com/articles/index-insider---1h26-isg-index-recap)). Kotak adds that deflation first shows up in renewals of large multi-year contracts once clients are in production, not in GenAI pilots ([Tribune/ANI](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/business/it-services-growth-to-stay-range-bound-at-3-per-cent-as-ai-deflation-pressures-margins-mid-tiers-better-placed-says-kotak/)).

The market-level picture shows a wedge between bookings and revenue. ISG's 1H26 index has ITO managed-services ACV **down 6%** while BPO rose 47%. Enterprises are consolidating providers through "larger, longer cost-takeout deals" ([ISG](https://isg-one.com/articles/index-insider---1h26-isg-index-recap)). HFS notes healthy pipelines at TCS, Infosys and Wipro but weak revenue conversion because of AI-led deflation ([HFS Research](https://www.hfsresearch.com/news/ai-drives-it-contract-renegotiations-within-24-months-as-pricing-models-shift/)). Everest Group's CEO summarised it as "a desperate market for the service providers" ([Reuters via Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/business/indian-sub-continent/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-462989)). The evidence on contract length conflicts. ISG sees longer consolidation deals, while Persistent reports some firms shortening tenure because of AI uncertainty.

The thin spots are significant. **No vendor discloses the annual percentage in its productivity-commitment clauses.** No source names a renegotiated contract with a disclosed price change. The global SIs (Accenture, Capgemini, IBM Consulting, Deloitte, EPAM) publish nothing usable on pricing-model mix. Jefferies, Nomura and Everest pricing data were not accessible.

## Rate cards hold while effort-priced annuities take the hit

The question that matters most for the model has the weakest evidence. **No 2024-2026 time series of T&M or staff-augmentation rate cards was found**, and the paywalled ISG, Everest and Gartner benchmarks could not be checked. The one direct statement is the Infosys CFO's: pricing "has remained stable" while volumes softened ([Zerodha Daily Brief](https://thedailybrief.zerodha.com/p/has-ai-broken-the-indian-it-model)). The only published 2026 staff-augmentation rate benchmarks are a negotiation-advisory blog's estimates, with no method and no year-on-year comparison ([The Negotiation Experts](https://thenegotiationexperts.com/blog/it-staff-augmentation-rate-negotiation/)). No source shows an explicit "AI-adjusted" rate card. On balance, deflation is arriving through fewer billable people per unit of scope, not through lower prices per person.

That does not mean effort-priced work escapes pressure. HFS says most application and infrastructure annuity contracts **are still priced on effort, headcount, ticket volumes and T&M**, and that these are the contracts where talks are moving toward productivity commitments, outcome-based pricing and gain-sharing ([HFS Research](https://www.hfsresearch.com/news/ai-drives-it-contract-renegotiations-within-24-months-as-pricing-models-shift/)). HFS's survey of 510 enterprise decision makers found **54% of AI-enabled services still bought on an effort or input basis**, 26% outcome-based with shared revenue, and 20% transaction or output-based. Buyers' stated preferences tilt toward outcome-based and hybrid models, with T&M apparently ranked lowest, although the mapping of figures to labels in the PDF cannot be fully recovered ([HFS webinar](https://www.hfsresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf/HFS-Webinar-Buying-SaS_21-07-2026.pdf)). Gartner advises procurement teams to pursue structural terms such as outcome-tied pricing and "fixed multipliers on rate cards" rather than discounts, though that advice targets AI vendors ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/8085665)). So the forward pressure falls on effort-priced annuities through headcount and productivity glide paths, even while the per-head rate holds.

Aggregate contract mix is barely moving. Infosys is the only firm found disclosing a split. **Fixed-price revenue was 54% in both FY2025 and FY2026** ([Infosys Form 20-F FY2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001067491/000119312526270520/infy-20260331.htm)), so whatever shift is happening sits inside contracts rather than showing up as a flip from T&M to fixed price. Back in July 2025, the Infosys CFO said outcome, pod or studio pricing would emerge but the "entire model" would not change within about a year ([Zerodha Daily Brief](https://thedailybrief.zerodha.com/p/has-ai-broken-the-indian-it-model)). Outcome-linked pricing is far more advanced in business-process work. Reuters reports **about 80% of TCS's finance, HR and business-services contracts** are tied to outcome measures, roughly double the late-2023 level ([Reuters via Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/business/indian-sub-continent/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-462989)). Among the global firms, Globant's output-priced AI Pods platform reached **$52.8M ARR in Q2 2026**, about 2% of its revenue run-rate, while total revenue stayed flat ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/globant-glob-ai-arr-jumps-61-in-one-quarter-to-52-8-million/)). Cognizant describes "outcome-based commercial arrangements" without giving their share ([MarketBeat](https://www.marketbeat.com/instant-alerts/cognizant-technology-solutions-q2-earnings-call-highlights-2026-07-29/)). Strategy firms are further along: BCG's CEO said three-quarters of its largest AI cases use variable fees ([WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/consultants-put-skin-in-the-ai-game-as-clients-demand-proof-of-results/)).

There is **no documented "pay what it would have cost you" contract** and no measure of how often clients accept one. The real variants are gain-share deals. Cognizant and Daimler Truck split AI cost savings. HCLTech's cloud deal with E.ON pays nothing in year one, then ties payments to efficiency gains and business outcomes ([Reuters via Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/business/indian-sub-continent/ai-reshapes-indias-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less-462989)). Mphasis sells "savings-led" deals so it does not "have to sacrifice profitability", and says live sandbox demonstrations are replacing RFPs ([Zerodha Daily Brief](https://thedailybrief.zerodha.com/p/has-ai-broken-the-indian-it-model)). A short news summary says TCS passes on "up to 15%" of AI savings, but it cites no executive or document ([NewsBytes](https://www.newsbytesapp.com/news/business/tcs-offers-outcome-based-ai-contracts-will-migrate-to-fixed-price/tldr)). About 80% of HFS respondents say responsibility is unclear when an AI partner gets something wrong, which is a practical barrier to outcome contracts ([HFS webinar](https://www.hfsresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf/HFS-Webinar-Buying-SaS_21-07-2026.pdf)). Infosys's "passed back largely" and Persistent's 25-30% ask both indicate that counterfactual-cost pricing does not survive competitive bidding, and that partial gain-share is the realistic form to model. Finally, no survey links a client preference for fixed price to T&M AI projects that never reached production. The pressure to show results is real but documented only through anecdote, such as West Monroe's "I feel a lot of client pressure on it" ([WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/consultants-put-skin-in-the-ai-game-as-clients-demand-proof-of-results/)).

## Past automation waves cut hours a decade before they cut rates

Across nine historical cases, the unit being priced decides who keeps the gain and how fast prices move. Where work is billed by input (hours, heads), productivity reaches the client immediately as fewer hours, and rates stay sticky or even rise. Where work is priced per unit of output (per word, per page), buyers turn a measurable proxy into a contractual discount within a few years. Where price is tied to the value of the outcome (a percentage of construction cost), vendors keep the gain as margin for a decade or more. And where the client can cheaply run the tool with its own staff, the bought-in service disappears rather than being repriced.

### Legal: hours collapsed, the hourly rate kept climbing

E-discovery is the cleanest case of hours collapsing. RAND found document review was a **median 73% of e-discovery production cost**, mostly billed by outside counsel, and judged that technology-assisted review (TAR) could cut attorney review hours by about 80% and total review cost by about 75% ([RAND MG1208](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monographs/MG1208.html); [RAND RB9650](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9650.html)). Just as important, by 2012 RAND judged contract-attorney rates had already "bottomed out". Labour arbitrage was exhausted before automation started removing hours, the same order IT services went through with offshoring before AI. By 2025, remote managed review still cost mostly **$25-$40 per reviewer hour**. TAR had become a per-GB or bundled platform feature, with 39% of buyers on "alternative" models ([ComplexDiscovery 2025](https://complexdiscovery.com/the-human-core-of-ediscovery-review-services-in-the-summer-2025-pricing-survey/)). The price of a reviewer hour fell in real terms, but the big saving came from fewer hours, and value moved to platform owners. Review was only about 4% of total lawyer hours, which kept the aggregate effect small ([Legal Futures on Remus and Levy](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/study-finds-ai-other-technology-less-of-a-threat-to-lawyers-jobs-than-believed/print/)).

The billable hour itself barely moved over the period. In 2019, non-hourly fees were a **median 6-10% of law-firm revenue**, or 11-15% at firms with 250 or more lawyers ([Altman Weil 2019](https://www.altmanweil.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Law-Firms-in-Transition-2019-An-Altman-Weil-Flash-Survey-.pdf)). In 2025, roughly 90% of corporate legal dollars still went through standard hourly arrangements. Worked rates **rose 7.3%**, the fastest since at least the financial crisis, while demand grew 1.9% and average firm profit rose 13%, all during near-universal GenAI adoption ([TRI 2026](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/01/2026-State-of-the-US-Legal-Market.pdf)). Law firms explained why change was slow: partners resist (69%), firms are not feeling enough economic pain (66%), and "clients aren't asking for it", a view held by 55-63% of firms every year from 2015 to 2019 ([Altman Weil 2019](https://www.altmanweil.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Law-Firms-in-Transition-2019-An-Altman-Weil-Flash-Survey-.pdf)). Pressure is building only now, and only from sophisticated repeat buyers. In 2026, Citi asked bidding firms to explain their AI savings and Morgan Stanley planned more competitive bidding and fixed fees ([Modern Counsel](https://modern-counsel.com/2026/wall-street-wants-its-share-of-big-laws-ai-savings/)). The lesson for T&M is direct. Hourly pricing persists while vendor margins are healthy and scope is uncertain. Clients answer by moving work in-house, to alternative providers or to fixed fees, not by negotiating hourly rates down.

### IT offshoring: sticky rates, elastic volume, slow contract-mix drift

The offshoring wave shows how the same vendors and contract types behaved under a large cost shock. In late 2002, Infosys billed about **$137,300 a year per onsite person and $56,800 per offshore person**. Offshore rates fell 1.6% in the quarter while volume rose 10.5%, and the CEO said pricing pressure was "levelling off... it is all volume driven" ([Infosys Q3 FY2003 transcript](https://www.infosys.com/investors/reports-filings/quarterly-results/2002-2003/Q3/Documents/transcript-earnings230.pdf)). Raw wage gaps were 2-10x ([UC Berkeley](https://newsarchive.berkeley.edu/news/media/releases/2003/10/29_outsource.shtml)), the onsite-to-offshore billing gap was only about 2.4x, and clients saved **15-40% overall** ([Matloff, CACM](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2004/11/6375-globalization-and-the-american-it-worker)). Most of the arbitrage was therefore kept by vendors or lost to overheads rather than passed on as lower rates. Clients benefited mostly by moving work to cheaper heads, not by getting rate cuts.

Rates did fall in downturns. In 2009, a large US financial client sought rate cuts of up to **15-20%**, Forrester said customers wanted 10-30% savings, and tier-1 pricing and margins fell 2-4% ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/magazine/cover-story/story/indian-its-darkest-hour-245240-2009-04-30)). Gartner found new deals priced 5-20% lower, with smaller cuts on renegotiations ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/outsourcing-prices-falling-by-up-to-20-in-downturn-say-analysts)). Infosys's revenue per billed person-month fell 3.0% in FY2013 ([Infosys 20-F FY2013](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001067491/000106749113000024/form20f.htm)). Managed-services deals carried glide paths. ISG measured **10-30% cumulative unit-price cuts over five years** in infrastructure and found market prices fell about twice as fast as contracted prices, leaving deals "out of market by the middle of year two" ([ISG](https://migration.isg-one.com/articles-nb/articles/index-insider-infrastructure-outsourcing-prices-continue-double-digit-declines)). Some ADM clients saw costs roughly halved over five-year deals ([CIO/Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/691100/data-center-are-managed-services-the-39-holy-grail-39-for-app-outsourcing.html)).

Contract mix moved slowly and not always in the expected direction. Infosys's fixed-price share **fell from 30.0% to 26.7% over FY2005-07**, because T&M volume grew fastest during the boom ([Infosys 20-F FY2007](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001067491/000106749107000015/form20f.htm)). It then rose to about 40% by FY2011-13 ([Infosys 20-F FY2013](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001067491/000106749113000024/form20f.htm)), and TCS reached about 51% by FY2015 ([TCS fact sheet](https://www.tcs.com/content/dam/tcs/investor-relations/financial-statements/2014-15/q2/Presentations/Q2%202014-15%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)). That is about 3 percentage points a year once the shift began, triggered by the 2009 downturn rather than by the cost shock itself, and T&M never fell below about 45%. Incumbents copied the delivery model rather than cutting price: Accenture's India headcount overtook its US headcount in 2007, about 4-6 years after the Indian tier 1 firms reached scale ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1681051/india-will-be-biggest-country-for-accenture-by-august.html)).

### Translation: per-unit pricing absorbed discounts until the unit became nearly free

Translation shows what happens to output-priced work. Machine translation discounts were bolted onto the existing per-word fuzzy-match grid. In 2014, Translated.net calibrated post-editing discounts by raising the weighted word count until more than 75% of translators accepted. That cleared at **about 27% off for French and Italian, and nothing for German** ([MultiLingual 2014](https://multilingual.com/articles/post-editing-mt-is-it-worth-a-discount/)). By 2020, Nimdzi considered **35-40% off** reasonable for light post-editing. It warned that hourly quotes built from average productivity "misinterpret" the gains and that edit-distance pricing invites over-editing ([Nimdzi 2020](https://www.nimdzi.com/machine-translation-post-editing-how-much-is-the-fish-nimdzi-finger-food/)). By 2024-2026, UK post-editing paid "about a third less" ([CompanyData.com](https://companydata.com/blog/uk-translation-industry-decline)), and thinly sourced EU reports put it at about a quarter of the from-scratch rate ([Resultsense/Guardian](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-05-08-europe-translators-ai-deepl/)). Per-word pricing proved durable. **91% of LSPs still used it in 2024** ([Slator](https://slator.com/resources/how-much-does-translation-cost/)), but by 2025 only 15.1% said it still works, and Nimdzi warned that prices were "approaching the elastic part of the demand curve" ([Nimdzi 100 2025](https://www.nimdzi.com/nimdzi-100-2025)).

Who captured the gain is clear. Buyers took the price cut. Intermediaries with scale kept margin, with some LSPs showing shrinking revenue but stable or better profits. The long tail paid. Slator found **boutiques shrank 3.7%, with 41% declining**, while larger tiers grew mainly through M&A, and organic industry growth was about flat in 2024 ([Slator LSPI](https://tech.slator.com/?p=103315)). Interpreting, where output is hard to measure, stays hourly with rising rates ([Nimdzi 100 2025](https://www.nimdzi.com/nimdzi-100-2025)). The mapping to IT is close. Once a proxy like "AI-assisted story points" exists, buyers will try to convert it into a discount. Effort pricing holds best for advisory and architecture work, whose output is hard to measure. And boutiques without scale or a platform are most exposed.

### Design and print: value pricing shields, in-sourcing destroys

Architects priced as a percentage of construction cost, which separated fees from labour input. By about 2010, fixed fees built from hour estimates had become the dominant model, yet fees were still about 9-10% of construction cost for new build ([Architect Magazine](https://www.architectmagazine.com/practice/an-architects-fees-whats-your-time-worth_o)). That suggests CAD's gains were kept or reinvested in more design iterations for a decade or more, although no source puts a number on the pass-through. Typesetting is the opposite case. Once Macintosh, PostScript and PageMaker made the full stack affordable in 1985, **about 4,000 North American trade typesetting shops shrank to "only a few" by 1995** ([WhatTheyThink](https://whattheythink.com/articles/55522-day-typesetting-industry-died/)). Clients did the work themselves, and the per-page market vanished rather than being repriced. CAD software list prices *rose* from $1,000 to $3,750 over 1983-1992 ([Shapr3D](https://www.shapr3d.com/history-of-cad/autodesk-and-autocad)), which shows value moving to the tool vendor, much as AI platform and token spend may capture value from IT services today. None of the clerical cases (typing, bookkeeping, switchboards, tellers) produced a usable price series. The spreadsheet's unit-cost effect is documented only as a recalculation going from a full day to a couple of hours ([NPR Planet Money](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/528807590)).

## Labour adjusts at the entry gate, and the role mix moves before headcount

The employment record is more consistent than the pricing record. **The occupation doing the automated task rarely collapsed when the technology arrived.** Adjustment came through fewer new hires and a changing role mix, and aggregate employment in the occupational family often grew.

Offshoring cut US computer programmer and support-specialist jobs by more than 6% over 2001-2007. Total US IT employment still grew about 8%, from 3.54M to 3.84M, and software engineers, DBAs and IS managers each grew 22% or more ([BLS 2009](https://www.bls.gov/careeroutlook/2009/spring/art04.pdf)). Peak-hype projections, such as Gartner's 25% of US IT jobs offshore by 2010 or Blinder's 22-29% of US jobs offshorable, overshot by a wide margin ([Matloff, CACM](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2004/11/6375-globalization-and-the-american-it-worker); [Blinder](https://swh.princeton.edu/~blinder/papers/07ceps142.pdf)). Today's AI "jobs at risk" figures are best read as upper bounds on exposure, not forecasts. In law, full-time paralegals grew from about 254,000 in 2000 to 378,000 in 2025 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/LEU0254483600A)), and lawyer headcount rose 2.9% in 2025 ([TRI 2026](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/01/2026-State-of-the-US-Legal-Market.pdf)). Yet the average summer-associate class fell from 10 per office in 2022-2023 to 8 in 2025, and median offers per office hit an all-time low of four ([NALP 2026](https://www.nalp.org/uploads/PressReleases/NALP2026PerspectivesLawStudentRec_1.26.26.pdf)). Telephone operators are the best causal evidence. Each city cutover cut young women's operator employment **50-80% permanently**. Incumbents lost out (one-third less likely to still be operators a decade later), but later cohorts were absorbed by growing clerical work with no loss of employment ([Feigenbaum and Gross, QJE 2024](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/2024-feigenbaum.pdf)). All three cases point to junior intake as the first and largest margin, which is how the current model already behaves, with junior hiring settling near 60% of its 2024 rate.

Role composition shifts toward the professional who absorbs the task. About 400,000 bookkeeping-clerk jobs disappeared after 1980 while about 600,000 accounting jobs were added ([NPR Planet Money](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/528807590)). Drafters fell from 283,000 in 1998 to 180,200 in 2025, about **-1.7% a year**, as BLS says engineers and architects took over drafting ([BLS MLR 1999](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/1999/11/art5full.pdf); [BLS OOH Drafters](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/drafters.htm)). Typesetters and compositors fell from about 156,000 in 1940 to about 27,000 by 1998, while design occupations grew about 25% a decade ([BLS Bulletin 902](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/employment-outlook-printing-occupations-4351/fulltext); [BLS MLR 1999](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/1999/11/art5full.pdf)). Office and administrative support fell from 12.7% of US workers in 1980 to 6.8% in 2022. Pure typists shrank, while secretaries upgraded, going from 71% to 95% word-processor use between 1987 and 1992 ([Fork Lightning](https://forklightning.substack.com/p/the-past-present-and-future-of-office); [CS Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/1995/0725/25131.html)).

Demand elasticity decides whether the profession grows or shrinks, and it does not stay constant. Bank tellers roughly doubled from about 300,000 in 1970 to about 608,000 in 2006, about +2% a year, because ATMs made branches cheaper and deregulation let banks open more of them ([Conversable Economist on Bessen](https://conversableeconomist.com/2015/03/03/atms-and-a-rising-number-of-bank-tellers/); [Wikipedia, citing BLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_teller)). Mobile banking then removed the need for the branch itself. Tellers fell to 339,200 by 2025, about **-3.7% a year** from 2010, and BLS projects a further -13% to 2035 ([BLS OOH Tellers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm)). Translation followed the same arc on a compressed timeline. Through the neural MT era the market grew 5-6% a year while US translator employment and median pay ($57,090 to $59,940 between 2023 and 2024) still rose ([Slator on BLS](https://tech.slator.com/?p=105543)). Even so, each percentage point of MT use slowed translator job growth by about 0.7 points, about **28,000 "missing" US jobs over 2010-2023** ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/lost-translation-ais-impact-translators-and-foreign-language-skills)). After ChatGPT, writing freelancers on Upwork lost 2% of jobs and 5.2% of earnings within months ([WashU Olin](https://olin.washu.edu/about/news-and-media/news/2023/08/study-ai-tools-cause-a-decline-in-freelance-work-and-incomeat-least-in-the-short-run.php)). Over 70% of UK CIOL members reported lower 2024 volumes ([CIOL](https://www.ciol.org.uk/work-volumes-freelance-translators-interpreters)), and UK translation-firm closures overtook incorporations in 2025 ([CompanyData.com](https://companydata.com/blog/uk-translation-industry-decline)). Rising median wages can hide this, because the lower-paid, more automatable work leaves first.

Timing splits into two clocks. Tool diffusion and local cutovers are fast. AutoCAD went from about 1,000 copies in 1983 to about 300,000 by 1990 ([Shapr3D](https://www.shapr3d.com/history-of-cad/autodesk-and-autocad)). LLM effects on freelancers showed up within 6-14 months, and firm closures within 2-3 years. System-wide occupational change is slow: about 15-25 years from CAD to visible drafter decline, about 40 years from the first ATM (1969) to the teller peak, and almost 60 years for full Bell System dial conversion ([Feigenbaum and Gross](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/2024-feigenbaum.pdf)). Early CAD also showed a J-curve. Proficiency took 4.7-9.4 months, and in one aerospace firm about half of designers rarely used the 300 workstations they shared ([NRC 1994](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/2135/chapter/11)). That has a direct counterpart in ISG's 2026 "validation bottleneck", where AI adds review work before it removes work ([ISG](https://isg-one.com/articles/index-insider---1h26-isg-index-recap)). AI spreads as software, not physical capital, so the long historical lags are upper bounds, not central estimates.

## Offshoring, legal and translation fit best; switchboards and ATMs set the bounds

No single analogue covers everything, so each should be used for the question it answers best. The offshoring wave involved the same vendors, buyers and contract types, but it was a labour-*cost* shock that lowered the price of an hour. AI is a labour-*productivity* shock that lowers the number of hours, so offshoring tends to understate pressure on T&M volume. Legal services fit the professional-services pricing politics and the junior-pyramid dynamics best. Translation is the best guide to per-unit discounting and to how fast LLM-era effects arrive, though its measurable word output makes it a cleaner per-unit market than IT, so it probably overstates how deep and fast discounts go.

## The T&M exemption holds for rates but not for annuities

The current model applies client forward price pressure to fixed-price, managed-service and outcome-based contracts, but not to T&M or staff-augmentation rates. Those two types pass through only realised productivity, as fewer hours or fewer heads. The project's own contract-mix diagnosis notes that this asymmetry pushes vendors toward effort billing. In the Phase 7 calibration, effort-billed share rises to 2030 (Indian IT 40% to 42%, global SIs 27% to 32%, boutiques 62% to 72%) before falling back by 2040.

**Four lines of evidence support the exemption for the per-hour or per-head rate.** Infosys reports stable pricing alongside softer volumes. Legal hourly rates rose at a record pace during GenAI adoption. Offshoring-era rates were "sticky, volume driven" outside downturns. And hourly interpreting rates are rising even as per-word translation prices fall. In all four, the effort contract passes productivity to the client through volume, which is what the model's full pass-through on T&M and staff augmentation already captures. The model's double swing in mix also has a precedent: Infosys's T&M share rose during the 2005-07 boom and fixed price regained share at about 3 points a year after 2009.

**Four lines of evidence contradict a blanket exemption.** First, the contracts HFS says clients are reopening are mostly application and infrastructure annuities priced on effort, headcount, tickets and T&M. There, the forward pressure takes the form of productivity commitments and headcount glide paths rather than rate cuts, so a T&M framework deal behaves more like a managed service with a glide path. Second, buyers in every analogue turned expected productivity into terms for effort-priced work: hourly post-editing quotes built from average productivity in translation, client demands for AI-savings explanations at bid stage in law, and 15-20% rate-cut demands with 2-4% realised in IT's 2009 downturn. Third, buyers say they prefer outcome and hybrid models to T&M, and Infosys's flat 54% fixed-price share shows no sign of vendors successfully steering into effort billing. Fourth, the legal case shows that when hourly rates stay high, clients move work in-house or to alternative providers rather than paying more hours. The model already has this response through its sourcing logit, but it only works if T&M's cost advantage in that logit is not overstated.

Four changes would bring the model in line with the evidence. First, split effort billing into **short spot T&M and staff augmentation**, which keeps no forward anchor, a real rate drift of 0 to -2% a year and an optional one-off downturn shock of 2-6%, and **effort-priced annuity frameworks**, which carry a partial forward anchor. A reasonable starting weight for that anchor is 30-50% of the fixed-price anchor, or an explicit 2-5% a year productivity glide path. Second, add a client-side preference penalty against T&M in the contract-type choice, so vendors cannot steer freely into effort billing while buyers are asking for outcome and hybrid terms. Third, test `PriceHorizon` against the 4-7 year range implied by the 20% renewal step-downs and the 5-7 year bid assumptions. The current fit sits against its upper bound, which suggests the price target is absorbing pressure that belongs on annuity T&M. Fourth, report the TCV-to-revenue conversion ratio as an observable, since its decline is the clearest real-world trace of AI deflation. With these changes, the 2026-2031 rise in effort billing should shrink, most for boutiques, where no evidence supports a 10-point rise and translation suggests small suppliers are squeezed hardest.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves the key modelling variable from contract type to **pricing unit and who controls the productivity proxy**. Price pressure reaches every contract type eventually. What differs is the channel. Effort contracts lose volume immediately and pick up glide paths at renewal. Output-priced contracts get discount grids within a few years. Value-priced contracts keep margin until procurement switches to bottom-up hour estimates. So the T&M exemption is less a statement that T&M escapes pressure than a statement that its pressure arrives as fewer hours and productivity commitments rather than lower rates. A model that leaves T&M with no forward channel at all will make effort billing look like a refuge, and no historical case shows such a refuge lasting.

The bigger long-run risk in the analogues is not repricing but bypass. Typesetting shops, bank branches and, increasingly, translation agencies lost demand because clients could do the work themselves, not because vendors cut prices. For IT consulting, that channel is GCCs and in-house teams using AI directly. In the model, this means the sourcing logit and consumer AI adoption deserve at least as much calibration effort as the price anchors, and it means demand elasticity should decline over time rather than be held constant. Translation's shift from Jevons-style volume growth under neural MT to flat organic growth under LLMs took only about two years.
